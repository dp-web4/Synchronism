# Paleolithic Graphic Grammar Exploration Arc

**Started:** 2026-09-07  
**Status:** Active independent exploration  
**Scope:** Archaeology, semiotics, information theory, cognitive science, computational grammar  
**Relationship to Synchronism:** A pin in the map, not a claim of the Synchronism physical framework

## Why this arc exists

A discussion of Jean-Jacques D.'s STAR decomposition - State, Thing, Action, Relationship - and a speculative attempt to apply it to recurring Upper Paleolithic cave signs raised a narrower and more interesting question:

> **Do Paleolithic graphic systems exhibit discoverable grammar, and if so, what kind of grammar does the evidence actually support?**

The point of this arc is not to force Paleolithic signs into STAR, Synchronism, or any other preferred ontology. It is to ask what structure the archaeological evidence wants to reveal when semantic assumptions are minimized.

This belongs in the Synchronism repository because Synchronism has always been a map of connected investigations, not because this topic needs to validate or derive from the rest of the framework. The connection, if any, can emerge later. For now this is an independent empirical and conceptual exploration.

## Current provisional result

The first pass substantially strengthens the case for **formal/compositional grammar** in at least some Upper Paleolithic graphic traditions, while weakening the specific hypothesis that STAR's four semantic categories are the natural latent classes.

The strongest current picture is:

1. **Graphic production is non-random and conventionally structured.**
2. **Composition appears rule-governed at several scales:** within signs, among signs, among themes, and across panel/cave context.
3. The best-supported primitive operations look more like **repetition, concatenation/juxtaposition, superposition, and integration/recursive embedding** than alphabetic word order.
4. Large figurative themes often behave as **anchors/hubs**, with signs and secondary motifs behaving more like modifiers, operators, relations, or contextual articulation.
5. A static image does not by itself distinguish semantic categories such as **state** from **action**. That distinction requires grounded semantics, temporal evidence, or a successful predictive model.
6. The first quantitative probes do **not** reveal a privileged four-class decomposition that would independently support STAR.

The emerging working hypothesis is therefore:

> **Paleolithic graphic systems may be better modeled as typed compositional scene grammars than as strings of proto-words.**

This is intentionally a hypothesis, not a decipherment claim.

## Research questions

### Q1 - Is there syntax without known semantics?

Can motif/sign occurrence and composition be predicted from other elements of a panel or sequence strongly enough to justify the term **grammar** in the formal sense?

### Q2 - What are the primitive composition operators?

Candidate operations already suggested independently in the literature include:

- repetition, with or without variation;
- concatenation / juxtaposition;
- superposition;
- integration / fusion;
- recursive embedding;
- orientation and placement transformations.

The arc should test whether these operations compress and predict the corpus better than surface-shape categories alone.

### Q3 - Are there latent functional classes?

If sign/theme behavior is clustered without semantic labels, do stable role classes emerge across caves, periods, and corpora?

Possible roles might include anchor, modifier, operator, relation, quantity, event, state, or something we do not currently have vocabulary for. No class count is privileged in advance.

### Q4 - Does STAR earn explanatory value?

STAR is retained as one candidate factorization:

- Thing
- Relationship
- State
- Action

It must compete against unconstrained latent models and simpler baselines. The test is predictive/compressive utility, not whether a researcher can retrospectively place every symbol into one of four boxes.

### Q5 - How much structure is visual grammar versus social/spatial grammar?

Recent work indicates that cave placement, visibility, accessibility, technique, theme, orientation, and composition all carry structure. The "message" may therefore be distributed across a scene and its physical setting, not confined to the marks themselves.

## Epistemic rules

1. **No decipherment by resemblance.** A zigzag is not an action because it looks dynamic. A circle is not a state because it looks static.
2. **Behavior before meaning.** Classify signs by distribution, substitution, co-occurrence, composition, and predictive role before attaching semantic labels.
3. **Separate corpora unless justified.** Aurignacian mobile marks, Magdalenian parietal art, and pan-European sign databases are related evidence streams, not one homogeneous dataset.
4. **Preserve archaeological criticism.** Ambiguous tracings, uncertain associations, dating spans, and corpus-selection effects are part of the model, not inconvenient noise.
5. **Prefer held-out prediction.** A grammar that explains the same examples used to invent it is weak evidence. It should predict withheld panels, motifs, or associations.
6. **Negative results remain findings.** If four roles do not emerge, STAR loses. If no stable latent grammar transfers between corpora, that is equally informative.
7. **Do not use this arc as evidence for Synchronism.** Any later relation has to be earned independently.

## Tracker

### 2026-09-07 - Arc opened; first synthesis and probe

- Recovered a long-running structuralist/semiotic literature reaching back at least to Sauvet, Sauvet & Wlodarczyk (1977), which explicitly analyzed sign combinations without claiming translation.
- Found Sauvet & Wlodarczyk's 2008 formal-grammar result: 416 polythematic panels, five thematic classes, a few rewriting rules accounting for 98% of the observed figurative compositions.
- Found Dehaene's 2024 cognitive-science convergence: elementary geometry appears describable using point/line/curve primitives plus repetition with variation, concatenation, and recursive embedding.
- Revisited Bacon et al. (2023) as a semantic hypothesis but retained major published criticism of its corpus construction and sign/animal associations.
- Examined Intxaurbe et al. (2024) and Intxaurbe (2026), which provide open, reproducible spatial and co-occurrence datasets showing structured, hierarchical, non-random organization without requiring semantic interpretation.
- Added Bentz & Dutkiewicz (2026), whose information-theoretic analysis of 43-34 ka Aurignacian mobile signs shows deliberate, systematic, conventional use with complexity comparable in some statistical respects to protocuneiform, while remaining clearly distinct from writing.
- Ran a first aggregate-data probe on Bacon's published sequence counts and a first formal-feature clustering probe using published/open Intxaurbe variables. Both support non-random structure; neither independently selects four STAR-like semantic classes.

**Current direction:** build a corpus-first predictive grammar experiment. The first high-value target is minimum-description-length encoding of geometric sign construction plus held-out co-occurrence prediction.

## Current confidence, explicitly provisional

- **Formal/compositional syntax exists in at least some Paleolithic graphic systems:** high confidence.
- **Some nonfigurative signs act as reusable operators/modifiers rather than standalone referents:** plausible, moderate confidence.
- **The system resembles a typed scene/composition grammar more than alphabetic writing:** moderate-to-high confidence as a modeling frame.
- **STAR specifically captures the latent semantic roles:** low confidence at present.
- **Specific English-language translations of individual signs:** very low confidence absent stronger independent grounding.
